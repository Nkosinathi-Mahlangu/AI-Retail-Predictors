2 Business Objectives

2.1 Business Background
• The client environment is a fuel filling station operating a hybrid retail model: a forecourt, an attached convenience store, and an in-house food kitchen.
• Ordering decisions for both the store and the kitchen are currently made manually, based on staff experience and periodic stock counts rather than historical sales patterns.
• This is typical of small and medium filling-station operators in South Africa, who generally lack the budget or technical staff to implement enterprise-grade demand-forecasting systems, despite operating in an industry where demand is highly variable and perishable stock is common.


2.2 Business Objectives

• Reduce stockouts of fast-moving convenience and kitchen items by forecasting
demand ahead of high-demand periods (weekends, holidays, promotions).
• Reduce waste of slow-moving and perishable kitchen ingredients by aligning
order quantities with predicted, rather than assumed, demand.
• Replace manual, experience-based ordering with a data-driven decisionsupport process for store and kitchen managers.
• Provide early, automated alerts when predicted demand is likely to exceed
current stock levels, enabling proactive rather than reactive ordering.
• Improve overall profitability, customer satisfaction and product availability at the
filling station.

2.3 Business Success Criteria
• At least a 30% reduction in stockout incidents for the top 20 fast-moving store
and kitchen items within 6 months of go-live.
• At least a 25% reduction in measurable kitchen ingredient wastage (by value or
weight) within 6 months of go-live.
• Demand forecast error (e.g. Mean Absolute Percentage Error) below 15–20%
for key product categories.
• At least 80% of restocking decisions made by managers reference the system's
alerts or forecasts within 3 months of adoption.
• Measurable reduction (e.g. 50%) in manager time spent on manual stock
counting and estimation.

2.5 Constraints

•	Limited quality and completeness of existing historical sales data, since records have been kept manually.
•	Limited IT budget and infrastructure typical of a small/medium filling-station operator, with no dedicated in-house IT or data-science staff.
•	Fixed academic project timeline and scope, limiting the depth of model tuning and integration possible before the due date.
•	Dependence on an external weather API and its availability, accuracy and pricing.

2.6 Risks

•	Forecast accuracy may be low if historical data is sparse, inconsistent or poorly labelled — mitigated by data cleaning and starting with simpler, explainable models.
•	Managers may resist adopting a new system and continue ordering manually — mitigated by a simple interface, short training, and phased rollout alongside the existing process.
•	A single model may not generalise well across different stores, regions or seasons with different demand patterns — mitigated by per-store/per-category retraining.
•	Forecast quality will degrade over time if sales and stock data are not captured consistently — mitigated by lightweight data-entry validation and regular monitoring.
•	Data privacy/security risk where the system connects to point-of-sale data — mitigated by restricting access and anonymising data used for testing

2.7 Initial Assessment of Tools and Techniques

•	Language: Python, using pandas and NumPy for data preparation and cleaning.
•	Forecasting/Machine Learning: regression models (scikit-learn) and time-series methods (ARIMA/SARIMA via statsmodels, or Facebook Prophet) to capture seasonality such as day-of-week and promotional effects.
•	Visualisation and reporting: Matplotlib/Seaborn for exploratory analysis, and a simple dashboard (e.g. Streamlit or Power BI) for manager-facing alerts and reports.
•	Alerting logic: rule-based comparison of predicted demand against current stock levels, triggering a dashboard notification, email or SMS alert when a shortfall is predicted.
•	Optional conversational layer: a lightweight chatbot/softbot allowing a manager to ask, for example, “what is the predicted demand for bottled water this weekend?” using simple intent matching or a small NLP library.
